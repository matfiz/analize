Lecture 7

The Fourier transform

On the whole space, Fourier coefficients become a function of a continuous wave number. We prove inversion by inserting a Gaussian factor and then removing it. Inversion gives an isometry on rapidly decreasing smooth functions. Density extends this map to a unitary map of L2(Rd)L^2(\R^d)L2(Rd). The same Gaussian argument proves uniqueness for integrable functions.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Check Fourier signs and constants for translation, modulation and differentiation.
  • Prove that the Fourier transform preserves the Schwartz space.
  • Derive inversion and the unitary extension onto L2(Rd)L^2(\R^d)L2(Rd).
  • Prove convolution and uniqueness for L1L^1L1 functions.

7.2 Schwartz space and differentiation

Recall the multi-index notation: for α∈N0d\alpha\in\N_0^dα∈N0d​, ∣α∣=∑jαj|\alpha|=\sum_j\alpha_j∣α∣=∑j​αj​, xα=∏jxjαjx^\alpha=\prod_jx_j^{\alpha_j}xα=∏j​xjαj​​ and ∂α=∏j∂xjαj\partial^\alpha=\prod_j\partial_{x_j}^{\alpha_j}∂α=∏j​∂xj​αj​​.

Definition 7.2. This is the space used for density in Theorem 5.9. Multiplying a derivative by (1+∣x∣2)M(1+|x|^2)^M(1+∣x∣2)M and expanding that polynomial shows that each derivative decays at least as (1+∣x∣2)−M(1+|x|^2)^{-M}(1+∣x∣2)−M, for every integer M≥0M\ge0M≥0. Taking 2M>d2M>d2M>d proves that every derivative, and every polynomial times a derivative, is in L1L^1L1 and L2L^2L2.

For example, differentiating e−∣x∣2/2e^{-|x|^2/2}e−∣x∣2/2 repeatedly gives a polynomial times the same Gaussian. It belongs to S\SchS. By Fubini (Theorem 4.1) and Theorem 4.5, its transform is F(e−∣x∣2/2)(k)=e−∣k∣2/2. \FT(e^{-|x|^2/2})(k)=e^{-|k|^2/2}. 7.4 Plancherel and the unitary extension

A linear map between Hilbert spaces is unitary if it preserves inner products and is onto. Onto is a separate requirement from norm preservation.

Theorem 7.7 (Plancherel). The transform has a unique extension to a unitary map F:L2(Rd)→L2(Rd)\FT:L^2(\R^d)\to L^2(\R^d)F:L2(Rd)→L2(Rd). For every f,g∈L2f,g\in L^2f,g∈L2, ⟨f^,g^⟩=⟨f,g⟩,∥f^∥2=∥f∥2.\inner{\widehat f}{\widehat g}=\inner fg,\qquad \norm{\widehat f}_2=\norm f_2.⟨f​,g​⟩=⟨f,g⟩,​f​​2​=∥f∥2​. On L1∩L2L^1\cap L^2L1∩L2, the extension agrees almost everywhere with the integral transform. On general L2L^2L2 functions, the defining integral need not exist.

Proof. First take f,g∈Sf,g\in\Schf,g∈S. Expanding f^‾\overline{\widehat f}f​​, Fubini and inversion give ⟨f^,g^⟩=∫Rdf(x)‾(F−1g^)(x) dx=⟨f,g⟩.\inner{\widehat f}{\widehat g} =\int_{\R^d}\overline{f(x)}(\FT^{-1}\widehat g)(x)\,\dd x =\inner fg.⟨f​,g​⟩=∫Rd​f(x)​(F−1g​)(x)dx=⟨f,g⟩. Fubini is justified by ∥f∥1∥g^∥1<∞\norm f_1\norm{\widehat g}_1<\infty∥f∥1​∥g​∥1​<∞. In particular the transform preserves the L2L^2L2 norm on S\SchS.

Next let f∈L2f\in L^2f∈L2. Choose fm∈Sf_m\in\Schfm​∈S with fm→ff_m\to ffm​→f in L2L^2L2, using Theorem 5.9. The isometry makes (f^m)(\widehat f_m)(f​m​) Cauchy in L2L^2L2. Completeness supplies a limit, which defines Ff\FT fFf. If another sequence approximates fff, the norm of the difference of the transforms equals that of the approximants and tends to zero. The extension is therefore well-defined and unique. Passing to limits in inner products proves the asserted identity for all f,g∈L2f,g\in L^2f,g∈L2.

Its range is closed: a convergent sequence Ffm\FT f_mFfm​ makes fmf_mfm​ Cauchy by the norm identity, and its limit maps to the given limit. Inversion on S\SchS shows that the range contains S\SchS. This subspace is dense in L2L^2L2, so the closed range is all of L2L^2L2.

Finally suppose f∈L1∩L2f\in L^1\cap L^2f∈L1∩L2. Truncate it to f1{∣x∣≤m, ∣f∣≤m}f\one_{\{|x|\le m,\,|f|\le m\}}f1{∣x∣≤m,∣f∣≤m}​ and convolve the truncation with a compactly supported smooth mollifier from Lecture 5. Choose the mollifier scale so the error is below 1/m1/m1/m in both L1L^1L1 and L2L^2L2, using Theorem 5.8 for each norm. The resulting functions gmg_mgm​ are compactly supported and smooth: bounded derivatives of the mollifier may be passed under the integral against the compactly supported L1L^1L1 truncation. They tend to fff in both norms, because truncation does so by dominated convergence. Their integral transforms converge uniformly to the integral transform of fff, by the L1L^1L1 bound. Their transforms also converge in L2L^2L2 to the extension. An almost-everywhere convergent subsequence of the latter convergence, supplied by Theorem 5.3, identifies the two limits. 

For example, the self-dual Gaussian has the same norm in position and wave number. More generally, Plancherel defines wave-number amplitudes even for square-integrable functions that are not integrable. QM-II proves the position–momentum uncertainty relation on Schwartz functions in its uncertainty theorem. This result is not used here.

7.5 Convolution and uniqueness

Theorem 7.8. For f,g∈L1(Rd)f,g\in L^1(\R^d)f,g∈L1(Rd), the convolution is integrable and f∗g^=(2π)d/2f^ g^.\widehat{f*g}=(2\pi)^{d/2}\widehat f\,\widehat g.f∗g​=(2π)d/2f​g​. Theorem 7.9 (Uniqueness in L1L^1L1). If f∈L1(Rd)f\in L^1(\R^d)f∈L1(Rd) and f^=0\widehat f=0f​=0, then f=0f=0f=0 almost everywhere.

Proof. The regularisation identity (7.4) holds for every L1L^1L1 function, independently of inversion. If f^=0\widehat f=0f​=0, it gives f∗Hε=0f*H_\varepsilon=0f∗Hε​=0 at every point. By Theorem 5.8, these convolutions tend to fff in L1L^1L1. Hence ∥f∥1=0\norm f_1=0∥f∥1​=0, as required. 

The regularisation identity serves two different conclusions. For a Schwartz function, uniform convergence of the convolutions yields the pointwise inversion formula. For an arbitrary integrable function, convergence in integral norm is enough for uniqueness. This second argument never needs a pointwise inverse integral for that function.

In particular, two integrable functions with the same transform agree almost everywhere, by applying uniqueness to their difference.

The unitary extension discharges QM-II’s standing fact S1. Poisson summation and its proof will be given in Lecture 22.

Summary

  • Gaussian regularisation proves inversion on S\SchS and uniqueness on L1L^1L1.
  • Inversion makes the L2L^2L2 extension onto, as well as an isometry.
  • For two L1L^1L1 functions, unnormalised convolution contributes the factor (2π)d/2(2\pi)^{d/2}(2π)d/2 to the transform of their convolution.
  • The transform preserves Schwartz space continuously in both directions.
