Lecture 19

Double covers and genus

What this lecture does

A square root has two values over most points of the plane. Continuing a chosen value around a zero can exchange them. We construct a surface on which the square root is single-valued. Its points over infinity complete the construction. Cutting and gluing then explains its genus, the number of handles, at the stated sketch level.

19.1 Continuation of a square root

Fix a polynomial PPP of degree d≥1d\ge1d≥1 whose zeros are distinct. A local branch of its square root on an open set is a holomorphic function yyy there with y(z)2=P(z)y(z)^2=P(z)y(z)2=P(z). On a sufficiently small disk about a point where P≠0P\ne0P=0, such a branch exists. Shrink the disk until PPP is nonzero there. The function P′/PP'/PP′/P has a primitive LLL by Theorem 9.4. Choose its additive constant so that eL=Pe^L=PeL=P at the centre. Since (Pe−L)′=0(Pe^{-L})'=0(Pe−L)′=0, this equality holds throughout the disk. Thus LLL is a local logarithm of PPP, and exp⁡(L/2)\exp(L/2)exp(L/2) is a branch. Its negative is the other branch. Any two branches with the same value at one point agree on a smaller connected disk, since their quotient takes values in {1,−1}\{1,-1\}{1,−1} and is continuous.

A path here is a continuous map from [0,1][0,1][0,1] into the plane. A germ records a holomorphic function near one point, identifying functions that agree on a smaller neighbourhood. To continue a germ along a path is to choose local functions along it that agree on overlaps. This recalls the extension through an interval in Lecture 12; the construction here concerns square roots.

Proposition 19.1. A square-root germ continues uniquely along any fixed path avoiding the zeros of PPP. A small positive circuit about one simple zero exchanges the two germs. Two circuits restore the initial germ.

Proof. The compact path is covered by finitely many disks on which the preceding branches exist. Subdivide the path so that each piece lies in one disk. Choose the sign on successive disks to agree at a common point. Local uniqueness makes these choices agree near that point, so they give continuation. A common refinement of two such subdivisions and the same local uniqueness prove uniqueness along the fixed path.

At a simple zero aaa, factor P(z)=(z−a)Q(z)P(z)=(z-a)Q(z)P(z)=(z−a)Q(z), where Q(a)≠0Q(a)\ne0Q(a)=0. Choose a holomorphic root HHH of QQQ on a small disk. On z=a+reitz=a+re^{\ii t}z=a+reit the continued root is reit/2H(a+reit)\sqrt r e^{\ii t/2}H(a+re^{\ii t})r​eit/2H(a+reit), after fixing its initial sign. Increasing ttt by 2π2\pi2π reverses that sign. Increasing it by 4π4\pi4π restores it. 

The change of a germ after continuation around a closed path is called monodromy. The proposition proves the local square-root case. It does not assert a general monodromy theorem for arbitrary germs. For example, a root of z(z−1)z(z-1)z(z−1) changes sign around zero alone. On a large circle enclosing both zeros each of its two square-root factors changes sign, so their product returns to its initial value.

19.2 A regular surface at a branch point

A Riemann surface is a surface with complex local coordinates whose changes of coordinate are holomorphic with holomorphic inverses. A surface has a disk neighbourhood at each point; the coordinates identify that neighbourhood with an open disk in C\CC. Our surface begins with the set of pairs {(z,y)∈C2:y2=P(z)}.\{(z,y)\in\C^2:y^2=P(z)\}.{(z,y)∈C2:y2=P(z)}. The projection pr⁡\operatorname{pr}pr sends (z,y)(z,y)(z,y) to zzz. Where P(z)≠0P(z)\ne0P(z)=0, the two local branches give two disjoint coordinate neighbourhoods, each with coordinate zzz. These are the two sheets over a small disk.

A branch point of the projection is a point where its expression in a surface coordinate is not locally one-to-one. Here uuu and −u-u−u project to the same zzz. At u=0u=0u=0 the two sheets join in one regular surface point. Its image aaa is a branch value on the base plane. For the curve y2=zy^2=zy2=z, the coordinate u=yu=yu=y covers the entire affine surface. The apparent difficulty at zero belongs to the projection, not to the surface.

19.3 The points over infinity

To compactify this surface is to add its missing points at infinity with regular coordinate neighbourhoods, obtaining a compact surface. The coordinate on the base sphere near infinity is w=1/zw=1/zw=1/z. Write the nonzero leading coefficient of PPP as AAA.

Proposition 19.3. If d=2g+2d=2g+2d=2g+2, there are two points over infinity and neither is branched. If d=2g+1d=2g+1d=2g+1, there is one point over infinity and it is branched. Here g≥0g\ge0g≥0; the topological meaning of ggg follows below.

Denote the resulting compact surface by XP\mathcal X_PXP​. The function yyy is holomorphic on its affine part and meromorphic at infinity: it has poles there. A cubic has one point over infinity. A quartic has two. Both will have genus one, but their projections differ at infinity.

19.4 Cutting and genus

A cut is an arc joining two branch values on the base sphere. Choose pairwise disjoint cuts pairing all branch values, including infinity when necessary. Take two copies of the sphere cut along these arcs. Cross-gluing identifies one bank of a cut on the first copy with the opposite bank on the second copy. The local square-root charts describe precisely these identifications at the endpoints.

The diagram shows the two cuts for a quartic cover. The matching arrows record cross-gluing, not another projection branch point.

Each cut has two banks. Cross-gluing joins opposite banks on different sheets. Two cuts produce the genus-one case of the gluing sketch.

The complex coordinates orient the surface: counter-clockwise orientation in one coordinate agrees with that in the next. A closed surface is connected if it cannot be separated into two disjoint nonempty open parts. This cover is connected because continuation around any one branch point joins its two sheets. Its genus is the number of handles in its description as a sphere with handles. The classification behind this description is a topological theorem, used here without proof.

Proposition 19.4 (Genus sketched). For a polynomial of degree d≥1d\ge1d≥1 with distinct zeros, the number BBB of branch values and the genus of XP\mathcal X_PXP​ are B=d+(d mod 2),g=B−22=⌊d−12⌋.B=d+(d\bmod2),\qquad g=\frac{B-2}{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{d-1}{2}\right\rfloor.B=d+(dmod2),g=2B−2​=⌊2d−1​⌋.

Gluing sketch. The infinity charts give the stated branch count, which is even. There are B/2B/2B/2 paired cuts. Joining the first pair of cuts connects the two cut spheres into one sphere. Each further cross-join adds a handle. There are therefore B/2−1B/2-1B/2−1 handles. This uses the topological description of surfaces; the local holomorphic charts alone do not prove it. See Dubrovin, pp. 58–60. 

For a cubic, B=4B=4B=4 because infinity is branched. For a quartic, B=4B=4B=4 without an additional branch value. Both give g=1g=1g=1. For degree seven, B=8B=8B=8 and g=3g=3g=3.

In Integrability’s periodic Toda chain, the base coordinate is the spectral variable λ\lambdaλ in place of zzz. A nonzero Floquet multiplier ζ\zetaζ is the factor by which a solution changes after one period. Its relation to the polynomial transfer trace T\mathcal TT is ζ+ζ−1=T(λ).\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=\mathcal T(\lambda).ζ+ζ−1=T(λ). Setting y=ζ−ζ−1y=\zeta-\zeta^{-1}y=ζ−ζ−1 gives y2=T(λ)2−4y^2=\mathcal T(\lambda)^2-4y2=T(λ)2−4. Coincident zeros violate the hypothesis; the present smooth-cover construction does not cover that case.

Summary

  • A branch point of the projection is a regular surface point. The projection is locally given by z=a+u2z=a+u^2z=a+u2.
  • Infinity is branched exactly for odd degree; g=⌊(d−1)/2⌋g=\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloorg=⌊(d−1)/2⌋ is the sketched genus formula.
