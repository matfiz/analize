Lecture 20

The Weierstrass function

A meromorphic function can repeat in two independent complex directions. We construct such a function by a convergent sum over a lattice. Its poles determine a cubic differential equation. The equation relates the lattice description to a genus-one curve. The analytic construction and differential equation are proved; the final geometric identification is a sketch.

By the end of this lecture you will be able to:

  • Justify the subtracted lattice series by an explicit convergence bound.
  • Prove evenness and periodicity of the Weierstrass function.
  • Derive its differential equation and identify its half-period values.
  • Locate the point at infinity in the torus–cubic sketch.

20.2 Liouville theorems for elliptic functions

The order of a nonconstant elliptic function is the number of its poles in one cell, counted with multiplicities and with identified edges counted only once. For a nonconstant function a shifted cell can avoid zeros and poles on its boundary: there are finitely many such points modulo Λ\LambdaΛ, by isolation of zeros and poles and compactness of the cell.

Theorem 20.1. A holomorphic elliptic function is constant. The sum of the residues of an elliptic function in one cell is zero. A nonconstant elliptic function has equally many zeros and poles in one cell, counted with multiplicity. In particular it cannot have exactly one simple pole modulo Λ\LambdaΛ.

Proof. A holomorphic elliptic function is bounded on the compact cell and hence on the plane. Liouville’s theorem, Theorem 10.4, makes it constant.

Choose a translated cell with no boundary pole. The integrals of fff over opposite edges cancel because those edges differ by a period and have opposite boundary orientations. The residue theorem (Theorem 11.3) gives a zero sum of residues. A lone simple pole would have nonzero residue, which is impossible.

For nonconstant fff, choose the boundary to avoid its zeros as well. The function f′/ff'/ff′/f is elliptic, so its opposite-edge integrals also cancel. The argument principle, Theorem 11.5, identifies its integral divided by 2πi2\pi\ii2πi with the number of zeros minus the number of poles. Thus that difference is zero. 

For example, an elliptic function with one double pole and no other poles has two zeros counted with multiplicity. The same holds for f−cf-cf−c for every c∈Cc\in\Cc∈C, since subtracting a constant does not change the poles. The construction below realises precisely this pole pattern.

20.6 The torus and the cubic

The quotient C/Λ\C/\LambdaC/Λ is the set of equivalence classes under z∼z+ℓz\sim z+\ellz∼z+ℓ. Its coordinates come from small disks that do not meet their nontrivial lattice translates. Opposite edges of a cell are identified, so the quotient is a compact torus, a genus-one surface. This is the same topological gluing interpretation used in Lecture 19.

Summary

The subtracted lattice sum converges normally and defines an even, doubly periodic meromorphic function. Its Laurent coefficients determine the cubic ODE. The torus–cubic interpretation retains its geometric sketch status.

  • Absolute convergence justifies reindexing the derivative series to prove periods.
  • The half-period values are the distinct roots of 4X3−g2X−g34X^3-g_2X-g_34X3−g2​X−g3​.
  • A lattice point corresponds to the cubic’s point [0:1:0][0:1:0][0:1:0] at infinity.
