Bibliography

The textbooks the course follows, and then every work each lecture cites — books with the chapter or section the lecture uses, and the original papers.

The books

The following books support the course’s approved design. Each written lecture ends with the chapters and pages used in that lecture.

  • S. Axler, Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, Springer GTM 282, 2020. Lebesgue measure, convergence, product integration and LpL^pLp spaces, §§2D, 3A–3B, 5B and Chapter 7.

  • R. S. Strichartz, The Way of Analysis, revised edition, 2000. Uniform convergence, power series and the contraction principle.

  • W. Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis, third edition, 1976. Uniform limits and differentiation of series, Chapter 7.

  • V. A. Zorich, Mathematical Analysis II, second edition, 2016. Uniform convergence, Chapter 16, and contractions, §9.7.

  • E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Princeton University Press, 2003. Complex derivatives and Cauchy’s theorem: Chapters 1–2.

  • E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton University Press, 2003. Fourier series and the Fourier transform.

  • E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Real Analysis, Princeton University Press, 2005. Integration and Hilbert spaces.

  • G. Teschl, Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, American Mathematical Society, 2012. Local existence and dependence on data.

  • W. Rudin, Real and Complex Analysis, third edition, 1987. Regular measures and continuous approximation, Chapter 3.

  • E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Functional Analysis, Princeton, 2011. Function spaces and tempered distributions, Chapters 1 and 3.

  • N. Jacob and K. P. Evans, A Course in Analysis IV, World Scientific, 2018. Fourier series and the unitary Fourier transform.

  • R. S. Strichartz, A Guide to Distribution Theory and Fourier Transforms, 1994. Further reading on distributions.

  • P. Blanchard and E. Brüning, Mathematical Methods in Physics, second edition, Springer, 2015. Distribution calculus and boundary values.

  • M. J. Ablowitz and A. S. Fokas, Complex Variables: Introduction and Applications, second edition, Cambridge, 2003. Cauchy integrals and asymptotic methods, Chapters 6–7.

  • M. Masujima, Applied Mathematical Methods in Theoretical Physics, second edition, Wiley-VCH, 2009. Principal values and dispersion relations.

  • R. Beals and R. Wong, Special Functions and Orthogonal Polynomials, Cambridge, 2016. Gamma asymptotics, Chapter 2, and descent methods, §13.5.

Lecture 2 · Ordinary differential equations: the mechanics contract

G. Teschl, Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, AMS GSM 140, 2012, §§2.2–2.6 and Theorem 6.1. R. S. Strichartz, The Way of Analysis, revised edition, 2000, Chapter 11.

Lecture 3 · The Lebesgue integral: measure and convergence

S. Axler, Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, Springer GTM 282, 2020, online version 16 August 2025, §§2D, 3A–3B. Fatou is Exercise 17 in §3A. E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Real Analysis, Princeton, 2005, Chapter 1, §§2–3. R. S. Strichartz, The Way of Analysis, revised edition, 2000, §§14.1 and 14.3.

Lecture 4 · Integrals with parameters and in several variables

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Real Analysis, 2005, Chapter 2, §3. R. S. Strichartz, The Way of Analysis, revised edition, 2000, §§10.1.4 and 15.2.3–15.2.4. S. Axler, Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, 2020, §5B.

Lecture 5 · LpL^pLp spaces, convolution and smoothing

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Functional Analysis, Princeton, 2011, Chapter 1, §§1–2. W. Rudin, Real and Complex Analysis, 3rd ed., 1987, Theorems 3.11–3.14. E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Real Analysis, 2005, Chapter 2, Corollary 3.7 and Proposition 3.9. Further reading: R. S. Strichartz, The Way of Analysis, revised edition, 2000, §7.5.2 and §14.4.

Lecture 6 · Fourier series

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis: An Introduction, Princeton, 2003, Chapter 2, pp. 42–54. N. Jacob and K. P. Evans, A Course in Analysis IV, World Scientific, 2018, Chapter 5, pp. 69–71. The Fourier coefficient and convolution conversions are stated in §6.2.

Lecture 7 · The Fourier transform

N. Jacob and K. P. Evans, A Course in Analysis IV, World Scientific, 2018, Chapter 11, pp. 173–179. Its equation (11.1) uses the same unitary normalisation as (7.1).

Lecture 8 · Tempered distributions and Green’s functions on the line

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Functional Analysis, 2011, Chapter 3, §§1.4–1.5 and Theorem 2.1. Their Fourier kernel is e−2πixξe^{-2\pi\ii x\xi}e−2πixξ; all constants here use our unitary convention. P. Blanchard and E. Brüning, Mathematical Methods in Physics, 2nd ed., 2015, §3.2 and Chapter 11, p. 163. That page writes 1/(x+i0)−1/(x−i0)=−2πiδ1/(x+\ii0)-1/(x-\ii0)=-2\pi\ii\delta1/(x+i0)−1/(x−i0)=−2πiδ, in agreement with our convention. Further reading: R. S. Strichartz, A Guide to Distribution Theory and Fourier Transforms, 1994, Chapters 2 and 4.

Lecture 9 · Complex derivatives and Cauchy’s theorem

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Princeton University Press, 2003, Chapter 1, §§2–3, and Chapter 2, §§1–2 (Goursat and primitives, pp. 34–39).

Lecture 10 · Cauchy’s formula and rigidity

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Princeton, 2003, Chapter 2, §§2, 4–5, pp. 37–41, 45–54. The boundary-cancellation proof above spells out the passage from local primitives for the finite line-and-circle contours used in this course.

Lecture 11 · Singularities and residues

J. Bak and D. J. Newman, Complex Analysis, third edition, Springer, 2010, §9.2, pp. 120–125. E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Princeton, 2003, Chapter 3, §§1–4, pp. 73–90. These sources’ unnormalised contour integrals are used as written. The Fourier conversion above uses the frozen unitary convention.

Lecture 12 · Cuts, Cauchy integrals and causality

M. J. Ablowitz and A. S. Fokas, Complex Variables: Introduction and Applications, second edition, 2003, §7.2, pp. 517–518. M. Masujima, Applied Mathematical Methods in Theoretical Physics, second edition, 2009, §§1.8 and 6.5.

Lecture 13 · The Gamma function and Laplace’s method

R. Beals and R. Wong, Special Functions and Orthogonal Polynomials, Cambridge, 2016, §§2.1–2.2 and 2.5–2.6, pp. 16–22, 26–31. Their Gamma variable zzz is written sss here. Theorem 2.5.2 and identity (2.5.7) are the cited source of the sectorial remainder, with the branch real on the positive axis. V. A. Zorich, Mathematical Analysis II, second edition, Springer, 2016, §17.3.3–17.3.5, pp. 438–441 (Beta, sphere area and radial power integrals), and §19.2, pp. 603–610 (Laplace localisation and its leading term). The source’s large parameter λ\lambdaλ is our sss; its maximised phase is our −φ-\varphi−φ.

Lecture 14 · Asymptotics of integrals

M. J. Ablowitz and A. S. Fokas, Complex Variables: Introduction and Applications, second edition, Cambridge, 2003, §§6.2–6.3, pp. 426–429 and 443–446. E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Princeton, 2003, Appendix A, §3, pp. 328–331. R. Beals and R. Wong, Special Functions and Orthogonal Polynomials, Cambridge, 2016, §13.5, pp. 342–345.

Lecture 15 · Möbius maps

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, 2003, Chapter 8, §2, pp. 218–223. Their disk factor and Cayley map are the negatives of ours. M. J. Ablowitz and A. S. Fokas, Introduction to Complex Variables and Applications, 2021, §5.7, pp. 350–358. Their cross-ratio ordering differs from the convention used here.

Lecture 16 · Harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis, 2003, Chapter 2, §5, Theorem 5.7, pp. 57–58; Complex Analysis, 2003, Chapter 8, pp. 215–216 and Theorem 3.1, p. 224.

Lecture 17 · Explicit meromorphic expansions and products

E. M. Stein and R. Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, 2003, Chapter 5, pp. 142–145, and Chapter 6, Theorem 1.7, pp. 166–167. Their reciprocal-Gamma proof uses general factorisation; the Beta-limit proof here is independent of it. E. Freitag and R. Busam, Complex Analysis, 2005, Theorem IV.3.1, pp. 223–227, for Mittag–Leffler existence.

Lecture 18 · Resolvents as analytic functions

G. Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, 2009, pp. 73–79 and Theorem 3.21, p. 108; only bounded resolvents and supplied-density inversion are used here. S. Axler, Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, 2025 version, Theorems 10.82, 10.93 and 10.106, pp. 318, 322, 329. The Friedrichs Schur derivation and local pole proof are given here in full.

Lecture 19 · Double covers and genus

Ablowitz–Fokas, Introduction to Complex Variables and Applications (2021), §2.3, pp. 61–67, illustrates branches and sheets; its nonprincipal argument choices are not imposed here. Dubrovin, Integrable Systems and Riemann Surfaces, pp. 58–60 and 64–70, supplies the stated topology and differential facts.

Lecture 20 · The Weierstrass function

Stein–Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, Ch. 9, pp. 262–274. Lawden, Elliptic Functions and Applications, pp. 149–158, confirms the half-period convention; its theta-first construction is not used here. Dubrovin, Integrable Systems and Riemann Surfaces, pp. 72–73, describes the torus–cubic map. DLMF 23.2, 23.3.9–10 and 23.12.1 fix the normalisations and the cited limit.

Lecture 21 · Jacobi functions and the pendulum

Lawden, Elliptic Functions and Applications, pp. 24–31, 36–37 and 114–115. DLMF 22.13 records the derivative equations; DLMF 22.5 records the hyperbolic endpoint. The proofs here use the real ODE and its invariants.

Lecture 22 · Theta functions and Poisson summation

Stein–Shakarchi I, Fourier Analysis, pp. 153–156, and II, Complex Analysis, pp. 284–291. DLMF 20.2.3, 20.7.32 and 20.13.1 use the argument πz\pi zπz relative to ours; their heat coefficient changes accordingly. No triple-product theorem or higher-genus theta formula is imported here.

Lecture 23 · Analytic differential equations

G. Teschl, Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, AMS, 2012, §4.1, pp. 111–114. C. M. Bender and S. A. Orszag, Advanced Mathematical Methods for Scientists and Engineers, McGraw–Hill, 1978, §§3.1–3.3, pp. 62–75. N. Jacob and K. P. Evans, A Course in Analysis, vol. IV, World Scientific, 2018, Chs. 20 and 22, pp. 335–339 and 379–382. The source uses the same Gauss parameters and Euler branch.

Lecture 24 · Regular Sturm–Liouville problems

N. Jacob and K. P. Evans, A Course in Analysis, Vol. IV, 2018 printing, Chapter 21, pp. 355–378, and Vol. V, 2020 printing, Chapter 18, pp. 503–514. Our leading differential sign is negative; the Robin energy argument above retains its endpoint terms explicitly.

Lecture 25 · The Newton kernel and outgoing waves

R. Strichartz, A Guide to Distribution Theory and Fourier Transforms, CRC, 1994, §§5.1–5.4, pp. 56–69. His forward transform has positive exponent and no unitary prefactor; our spatial transform has negative exponent and factor (2π)−3/2(2\pi)^{-3/2}(2π)−3/2. His Newton sign for Δ\DeltaΔ is reversed here for −Δ-\Delta−Δ. G. Teschl, Mathematical Methods in Quantum Mechanics, online version 12 February 2009, §7.4, pp. 171–172, formula (7.48), gives the same resolvent kernel with η=−z\eta=\sqrt{-z}η=−z​ on the decaying branch.

Lecture 26 · Hardy space and the Khalfin obstruction
